# Africa's Hidden AI Bottleneck

*Africa lacks a shared, testable definition of what good AI looks like under local conditions. That definition is what turns interest into demand, and demand is what makes every other AI investment pay back.*

**Date:** 22 November 2026  
**Reading time:** 7 min read  
**Author:** Njenga Kariuki

---

Anyone building AI across Africa knows the visible constraints: power, compute, local data, capital, and talent. All five are real, and serious teams across the continent are at work on each of them.

Alongside those five sits one that is harder to see: the lack of a shared, testable definition of what good AI looks like here. By good, I mean a measurably better result for the people served, at a cost that the business, public agency, or NGO serving them can sustain.

The AI industry already has a method for defining good: evaluations, or benchmarks. They are the tests on the scorecard an AI lab publishes when it releases a new model, and they are what labs optimize for. But the same job looks different in California and in Nairobi: how it is done, and what it costs. So Africa needs leading benchmarks of our own. That gives the AI labs, and every builder here, a clear target.

## What African AI builders say

A recent survey asked 85 African AI businesses, most of which sell to other businesses, what holds them back. The barrier they named most was customer trust and AI literacy: about two in three named it.[1]

**Figure: Share of businesses naming each barrier to scaling**

| Barrier | Share |
|---|---|
| Customer trust and AI literacy | 65% |
| High development and consumer costs | 49% |
| Integration with legacy systems | 34% |
| Long sales cycles | 33% |
| Data availability | 33% |
| Regulatory uncertainty | 24% |
| Talent availability | 16% |
| Currency volatility | 7% |

Source: GIZ and Briter, *AI Made in Africa*, October 2026. Survey of 85 African AI businesses. A business could name more than one barrier.

My reading: buyers can’t tell what will work. Builders can’t tell what test they have to pass. A shared, testable definition of good answers both, and that is what turns interest into demand.

Demand is also what makes the visible investments pay back. Capital follows contracts. Power grids and data centers take years to build, and they cannot wait for demand, but they pay back only when demand arrives. Infrastructure and demand have to compound together.

A definition of good is built at two levels: inside each organization, and across them.

## Build a test for each workflow

For a business, a public agency, or an NGO, the unit of value is the workflow: the way a job gets done to deliver value to the people it serves. Approving a loan is a workflow. So is triaging a patient at a clinic.

Every leadership team is asking where AI makes sense in its work. There should be an objective answer, and the way to get one is well defined. An organization codifies its most important workflows and builds a test for each one. The test holds real cases from its own work, the result its best people would accept, and what that work costs today. The cases stay private. The organization runs the test, and any supplier can take it.

Satya Nadella has said that private evaluations like these will become every company’s core IP.[2] They let an organization know when and where AI makes sense to apply. They also open the work to every builder. The smallest startup takes the same test as the largest lab, and whoever passes can win the work.

I built my first of these tests at Amazon in 2017, where I led product for how Alexa understood shopping requests. The top-line metrics said shopping worked. Each week I listened to 1,000 requests and judged each one myself, and only about half ended in the right offer. We wrote down what counted as success, built a team of ten who judged each request by the same standard, and sent each failure back to engineering. By 2018, 85 percent ended in the right offer.[3]

The lesson has been the same on every product since. Someone has to sit with the people who do the work, collect the cases, and agree what a good result is. You cannot define good from a boardroom.

It can start small, with 200 real cases from last month’s work, graded by the people who do that work best. And it does not need tidy systems. If the work runs on WhatsApp, phone calls, and paper, that is where the cases come from.

## Define good together

Beyond any one organization, we can deploy AI faster if we define good together. Industries, NGOs, and governments can agree on shared benchmarks for the conditions they have in common: phones with little memory, customers who switch languages mid-sentence, rules that change at each border. Then they can push AI suppliers to publish results against them. A supplier that wants their business has a reason to publish, and then to improve.

The method exists. The best-known example is OpenAI’s GDPval, which tests AI on real tasks from 44 US occupations, written and judged by people who do that work.[4] That is the right benchmark for the United States. We should use the same method for the work that drives our economies: mobile money and small-merchant credit, primary care, smallholder farming, classroom teaching. African researchers have already built benchmarks for our languages,[5] for medical knowledge,[6] and for AI safety.[7] I have not yet seen one built for our work.

No single institution can build that alone. Telcos, banks, and payment networks bring scale and real workflows. Frontline NGOs know how services reach people. Universities and research groups can hold the test cases out of public view, so that no model can memorize them.

## What to buy, and what to build and own

A definition of good also helps focus the decision on what to buy and what to build. Start with a question for any leadership team: if the model changed tomorrow, what would we keep? What we keep is what we know, written down: our data, the way we have redesigned the work, what we know about the people we serve, and above all, our definition of good. That is the part to build and own. An organization that owns its evaluations can test a new model the day it arrives, and move to it without starting again. So I would put one line into every AI contract: our data, our evaluation cases, and our records belong to us, and we can use them with any model.

The same question applies to a country. AI sovereignty means owning the definition of good, controlling the data and knowledge, keeping the right to verify and switch models, and running critical work locally. A country can hold all four without owning the factory that trains the largest general model. The right to switch is practical security: in June 2026, access to a leading AI model was suspended worldwide for 19 days under a US export directive.[8] A country that can switch, or run a model locally, keeps working.

My rule is two sentences. **If it gets cheaper without us, buy it, and stay free to switch. If it depends on our data and knowledge, build it, and own it.** The general model is what gets cheaper without us: by one estimate, a given level of AI capability costs five to ten times less every year.[9] Our markets change slowly: the phones people hold, the languages they speak, and what they can afford. What we build for them keeps its value.

I count five layers in the AI stack, from the computing at the bottom to the applications at the top. Applied to them, the rule says to buy the general models and to build almost everything else.

**Figure: What to buy and what to build, layer by layer**

| Layer | Decision | |
|---|---|---|
| Applications | Build them around our own workflows. | My priority for Africa |
| Delivery | Build AI for the phones people have, by voice, in their languages. | My priority for Africa |
| Knowledge | **Build and own** it: our languages, our rules, how our businesses and customers work, and our evaluations. | My priority for Africa |
| Models | **Buy** general models, ranked by our benchmarks. Build small ones for our languages and phones. |  |
| Infrastructure | Build the computing that has to run here, as demand arrives. |  |

My priority for Africa is the top three layers: applications, delivery, and knowledge. A general model becomes useful here through what we add above it. And those three layers create the demand that pays for the two below.

Knowledge and delivery are where I think Africa can lead. We have led on delivery before. Kenya did not invent the mobile phone. It built a service for local conditions, and Africa now accounts for two-thirds of the world’s mobile money value.[10] The global labs race to build the biggest models. **Our frontier is the most useful model that fits inside the phone people already own, in their languages, offline.**

## Two questions for leaders

For enterprise leaders: pick one of your most important workflows. If a small local team built something better next week, how long would it take you to know, and then to sign?

To know, you need the evaluation: build it for that workflow, and open it to builders. To sign, you need the steps agreed in advance. A builder that passes earns a paid pilot with real customers. Before the pilot starts, both sides agree in writing what the pilot has to show and what the contract is worth. A pilot that passes should end in a contract, not another pilot.

For public leaders, funders, and their partners: if a local team passed a shared benchmark next month, do your procurement rules give it a path to scale?

A ministry can use the same method for one public service: publish the benchmark, and let any builder show what it can do before a contract is awarded. Funders can move money from isolated pilots into shared benchmarks, which no single company or ministry will fund alone. Frontline NGOs can check that a tool which passes the benchmark also improves the result for the people it serves. Paired early with telcos, banks, and payment networks, they can turn that tool into a service that outlasts the grant.

## The people who build the tests

Building a definition of good is skilled work. It is the first step in getting AI to work inside an organization. Between May and July 2026, four of the largest AI suppliers announced about US$9 billion to place engineers inside their customers’ organizations.[11] That tells me where the hard part is, and where the jobs will be.

An organization keeps its definition of good, so the people who build it should be its own people, trained here. Chief executives can give their people time to build the test for one workflow, not only a license for the tools. When ministries and funders pay for shared benchmarks, they can have young engineers here build them. It is a skill those engineers can use at home and abroad.

## The stakes extend beyond Africa

By 2050, more than three in four net additions to the world’s working-age population will be in Sub-Saharan Africa.[12] The world cannot reach its full potential unless Africa reaches its own.

Buyers who can tell what works will buy. Builders who know what test to pass will build. That is how I think we accelerate AI production across Africa.
